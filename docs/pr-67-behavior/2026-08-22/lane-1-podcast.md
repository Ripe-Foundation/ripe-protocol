The Robinhood changes in this lane are most noticeable to three groups: borrowers whose collateral becomes unreliable, keepers and buyers who move a liquidation forward, and Stability Pool depositors whose capital absorbs part of it. The broad direction is more defensive accounting, more recoverable liquidation operations, and stricter conservation of assets. Those changes matter because they determine when a position is considered healthy, whether a stalled liquidation can restart, who pays which fees, and whether the Stability Pool accepts collateral it may not be able to value or safely hold.

Start with a borrower whose position is healthy. The core debt-interest formula, health ratio, liquidation thresholds, and equality boundary have not changed. Borrowing and withdrawing are still blocked once the account is marked in liquidation. The important change is how Robinhood treats collateral that exists in name but cannot safely support the position. The earlier behavior could skip zero amounts yet still retain weight for a positive balance whose value was zero. Robinhood marks unusable collateral as quarantined, makes the non-throwing health check report failure, and lets the Auction House decline affected terms. Separately, registered zero-balance collateral slots can now affect the highest and lowest loan-to-value terms.

Custody is part of that judgment. A Basic Vault previously reported a user’s nominal balance even when the vault’s actual custody had fallen below its aggregate nominal obligations. Robinhood preserves the asset identity but reports a safe amount of zero in that deficient state. A borrower may therefore look less healthy, and a liquidator may find that collateral excluded from selection. The exact behavior for positive-balance, zero-priced collateral is still unclear because a strict price lookup may revert before the health engine can return the quarantine signal. The under-backed vault path and non-raising health behavior are established.

There is also a configuration-sensitive exclusion for Stability Pool vaults. The canonical Stability Pool supplied no borrowing power before and still supplies none, so ordinary pool collateral has not suddenly lost borrow power. Robinhood additionally excludes every vault identifier configured as a Stability vault. That only creates a visible difference where a deployment has custom Stability vaults. Interfaces also receive a quarantine flag and must handle the expanded health result.

Now let the position cross the liquidation boundary. The high-level order is familiar: priority assets come before user-vault assets, and the usual deferred handling for the GREEN stablecoin, staked GREEN, and the Endaoment remains. Healthy canonical Stability Pool balances were already eligible as liquidation collateral and remain eligible, which prevents a common misconception that Robinhood newly exposes them. What changes is the selection standard. The earlier system selected against nominal balance. Robinhood selects against a safe, realizable amount, so under-backed, paused, or otherwise unusable cohorts can be skipped instead of being treated as available merely because an accounting entry exists.

Routing collateral into the Stability Pool is stricter as well. Previously, a same-vault and same-asset guard did most of the work. Robinhood asks whether the pool can accept the liquidation asset. Depending on state and configuration, the pool can reject a paused pool, a new pair when the active cohort is full, a reserved asset, deficient custody, or an asset without a usable price. Robinhood also rounds the pool gross-up to preserve the full required amount instead of using floor-style division. One edge is still unclear: the first priority phase can require a complete pool valuation before the later tolerant checks, and whether deployed priority configuration exposes that edge has not been established.

On the first liquidation pass, fee behavior changes in a way borrowers and keepers will feel. The earlier implementation could retain computed fees even when no auction started. Robinhood charges them only before the first pass that actually freezes collateral for an auction. An inert first pass carries zero fees, and a retry carries zero new fees. The keeper fee formula and caps are unchanged, but allocation is not. The Stability Pool spread now covers the base fee only. The keeper fee stays in the borrower’s debt, and the pool phase target is capped before allocation.

Robinhood also makes a stalled liquidation recoverable. Previously, the stored in-liquidation state blocked another pass. Robinhood permits a retry when no stored auction remains, even if the borrower is still unhealthy. Anyone can remove an active auction after it expires while the Auction House is unpaused. An unexpired, missing, or inactive auction simply returns false; calling while paused reverts. Together, expiry cleanup and fee-free retry give keepers an operational route out of a stale auction. One caution is established: the retry calculator can still show a fee-bearing hypothetical result even though an executable retry charges no new fees, so it is not a reliable executable quote.

If an auction does start, the discount curve now reaches its configured terminal discount at the last purchasable point, including an auction with a duration of one. The earlier discrete curve could end below that maximum, so a buyer now sees the configured maximum at the end. Manual auction starts remain restricted to the Switchboard authority, and the ordinary skip cases and refund of unspent buyer funds remain in place.

Purchase accounting is tighter. The earlier flow had no live-debt cap and accepted payment above the computed minimum, which could let excess repayment reach the borrower. Robinhood caps a purchase by current live debt, requires the computed spend, and returns unused batch GREEN to the caller. It rejects purchase entries that would receive no repayment credit. If collateral moves and the resulting payment is still zero, the entire batch reverts. Fee-charging, rebasing, or custody-deficient assets face exact balance and backing checks, so unusual token behavior is rejected rather than misaccounted.

The purchase surface is narrower even though batch use remains. The Teller no longer offers three single-action routes for buying an auction, claiming from the Stability Pool, and redeeming from it. Batch counterparts remain. The Stability Pool host also stops exporting two conversion helpers and its single-action claim and redemption methods, although the module still defines them. Integrations need supported batch routes where available. For delegated batch buys and redemptions, Robinhood avoids updating an unlocked, unpaused recipient’s action timestamp; batch claims still update the claimer’s. The Auction House still authorizes the Teller for a single auction purchase, but no alternative scoped Robinhood caller route was established, so the intended external path is still unclear.

After the liquidation work, restored health still clears stored auctions. A full repayment is easier to complete when collateral is unusable: once debt reaches zero, Robinhood skips further collateral traversal, clears the liquidation state, and emits empty term fields. The earlier path kept traversing collateral after the debt was gone, so bad collateral could obstruct a clean payoff. On a partial repayment with an empty traversal, Robinhood preserves stored debt terms instead of overwriting them. Repayment is less likely to be trapped by collateral that no longer needs to support debt.

For a Stability Pool depositor, stricter admission begins before liquidation. Robinhood rejects deposits of GREEN itself, assets marked as reserved, and deposits that would mint zero shares. Available principal is based on custody after subtracting reserved balances rather than raw custody. Where a preferred Stability Pool is configured, conversions target that nonzero preferred vault instead of assuming vault one. Core share-conversion math remains the same, so these are safeguards and routing changes rather than a new economic formula.

Claim assets now have an explicit lifecycle. The earlier pool activated them immediately and delisted them below a ten-cent value. Robinhood separates active and dormant cohorts, caps the active set at twenty assets, and applies guarded activation and retention floors. Dormant pairs leave active iteration and net asset value, but they remain explicitly claimable and redeemable. Active claim valuation now requires adequate aggregate custody and a nonzero price instead of silently skipping a zero-priced asset. That stricter valuation becomes visible only when a pool actually contains deficient or unpriced active claims.

Pool maintenance is more observable and more permissionless. New views report active count, claim-asset state, and whether the pool can accept a liquidation asset. Anyone can prune qualifying assets. Anyone can also call host activation while the contract is paused, subject to an empty active cohort and the applicable floor and capacity checks. It is still unclear whether callers have a supported route to activate one asset at a time. Claim and redemption flows verify exact receipt, a zero-share redemption preview can skip before mutating claim state, and automatic redeposit uses the pool’s actual vault identifier instead of assuming vault one.

Finally, claim-pair balances now count when deciding whether the vault has funds or can retire, not just the share balance. Governance recovery authority is narrower too. The recovery method names remain present, but in Robinhood both always revert, so the Switchboard can no longer use those inherited paths to move orphaned funds. That reduces an administrative escape hatch and leaves incident responders with fewer recovery options if assets become stranded.

The concise takeaway is this: Robinhood makes unreliable collateral contribute less, lets stale liquidations be cleaned up and retried without compounding fees, conserves buyer and borrower value more strictly, and turns Stability Pool asset handling into a guarded lifecycle rather than an implicit list. The three consequential questions still open are how positive unpriced collateral behaves through strict pricing, whether live priority configuration can reach the strict pool-value edge, and what supported external route is intended now that single-action host methods have disappeared even though some underlying functionality and authorization remain.
