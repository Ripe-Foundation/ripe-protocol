This episode uses an AI-generated voice. Robinhood changes the product most noticeably for people whose money movement is imperfect, delegated, or happening while collateral is unhealthy. Depositors and withdrawers get stricter accounting. Borrowers face clearer quarantine boundaries. Someone repaying for another user gets a more intuitive refund. And keepers or wallet integrations lose a few broad, implicit permissions. The theme is that Robinhood is less willing to guess, cap, or quietly continue when custody, valuation, or authority is ambiguous.

Start with deposits. In the prior behavior, the Teller could transfer tokens and then trust the amount the vault said it credited. Robinhood requires two exact results: the Teller must receive the full token amount, and the vault must credit that full amount. A fee-on-transfer token, a vault that reports a short credit, or any other mismatch now causes the deposit to revert instead of completing with a smaller or inconsistent result. For callback-capable tokens, Robinhood also creates a temporary receipt-measurement window that blocks an overlapping deposit or housekeeping action. That extra callback protection is configuration-dependent because the report did not establish which deployed tokens can actually call back or what permissions they have.

The stricter rule continues inside basic vaults. A deposit is accepted only when custody covers the vault’s existing liabilities plus the new amount. Previously, the vault could credit the lesser of the requested amount and available custody. Robinhood refuses that partial accommodation. If the vault is already under-backed, its usable-balance views return zero, even though nominal inventory information still exists. That distinction matters across the whole lane: the position is not erased, but it cannot support withdrawal, borrowing, redemption, or debt-repayment deleverage until the backing problem is resolved.

Share-based vaults also become more literal about value. A deposit that would produce zero shares now reverts, so tokens cannot leave the depositor without a corresponding position. On withdrawal, Robinhood measures what actually leaves the vault and what actually reaches the recipient. It credits the measured delivery and burns shares for the measured outflow, with a small tolerance for rounding. Share conversion also uses full-precision arithmetic, so a quote can succeed when the final answer fits even if an intermediate multiplication would previously have overflowed.

Several smaller deposit rules point in the same direction. An empty deposit or withdrawal batch now reverts immediately instead of returning zero after housekeeping. Held funds must fit the configured caps in full instead of being silently reduced to the cap. Ordinary deposit authorization and caps still apply, and trusted-deposit permissions keep the same shape. But omitted user access choices now default to deny for deposit and repayment, rather than defaulting to allow. A registered Lego acting for a user also needs that user’s specific grant before making a governance deposit.

Third-party activity changes the user experience in a subtle but important way. When someone deposits, repays, or redeems for a beneficiary, Robinhood checks the beneficiary’s lock state but preserves that beneficiary’s last-touch marker. Previously, completing the third-party action wrote a new last-touch. This reduces the chance that a helpful action performed by someone else unexpectedly resets a user-specific timing marker. Ordinary withdrawals still write last-touch, so this is not a general removal of the timing rule.

Two deposit destinations become configurable. Governance deposits use a configured, nonzero core governance vault, with the old destination as the default. Stability deposits and borrow-to-stability flows similarly use a configured preferred vault, again retaining the old destination as the default. The actual deployed vault identifiers are still unclear. The Ledger can also use either the ordinary block number or the Arbitrum system action block for last-touch timing. Source behavior is established, but the deployed action-block source is still unclear.

Now consider withdrawals. A basic vault no longer caps a withdrawal to whatever custody happens to be available. Robinhood requires the vault to be fully backed and requires exact decreases at the vault and exact delivery to the recipient. A mismatch reverts. For a borrower preserving collateral, the maximum-withdraw calculation rounds the collateral that must remain upward rather than downward. That is conservative by a small unit, but it can be the difference between a quoted maximum being safely withdrawable and leaving the account just below its required backing.

Quarantine creates a larger boundary. If collateral is registered and live but cannot be given usable value, Robinhood can quarantine the account. Borrowing is blocked and maximum borrow becomes zero. If collateral traversal detects a quarantined sister asset, maximum withdrawal can also become zero. The latter behavior depends on the deployed asset relationships and configuration. An under-backed basic vault reaches a similar practical result through usable balances: nominal holdings remain visible, but available collateral becomes zero.

Borrowing itself keeps familiar permissions and proceeds. Borrowing for another user still needs authorization, an account already in liquidation is still rejected, and the borrower still receives the borrowed amount less the existing fee. What changes is how collateral contributes to terms. Configured Stability vaults are excluded. A registered asset with a zero amount can still retain its weighted debt terms, while positive dust with no borrowing capacity can no longer set the account’s lowest loan-to-value threshold. And if no eligible collateral supplies replacement terms, Robinhood preserves the stored terms rather than overwriting them with an empty result. For a borrower carrying residual debt, that preservation can affect future accrual.

Repayment has one especially clear improvement for third-party payers. If the payer sends more than the debt requires, Robinhood returns the surplus to the payer. Previously, the surplus went to the debtor. Authorization and minimum repayment sizing remain the same, but the money now returns to the address that supplied it. As noted earlier, a repayment performed for someone else also preserves the debtor’s last-touch marker.

Repayment becomes more tolerant of price trouble without forgiving debt. A full payoff skips collateral repricing entirely. A partial standard repayment values collateral non-strictly instead of requiring every price to be usable. This means a user can reduce or eliminate debt during a pricing failure in cases where the prior behavior could revert. At full payoff, the local bundle of borrow terms is left empty, so the repayment event’s collateral fields become zero. That event consequence is established by the synthesis and may matter to indexers even though the debt outcome is correct.

Redemption is now batch-only; the singular redemption entry is removed. Within a batch, Robinhood is more selective about bad targets. It skips targets with unusable pricing and makes quarantined accounts non-redeemable, rather than letting one such target necessarily derail the whole attempt. It also predicts zero-credit dust before moving value and skips it. If a transfer produces excessive outflow, or sends a positive amount that earns zero debt credit, the operation reverts. These checks aim to prevent value from moving without the expected repayment effect.

Some redemption fundamentals remain unchanged. The system still skips the caller, debtless users, and users in liquidation. A batch in which every target is skipped still reverts, and the redemption payback calculation is unchanged. When redemption moves value through internal shares, Robinhood rounds the share transfer down and reports the value that can actually be realized, or zero. The earlier behavior rounded the share amount upward, which could overstate what the transfer represented.

Finally, deleverage becomes narrower and more user-specific. The singular deleverage entries are removed; batch and specific forms remain. A registered Underscore integration no longer receives blanket trust across an entire batch. Trust is resolved for each user. Ordering another user’s assets requires that user’s borrow permission. The withdrawal-based form also requires that permission, but once it is present Robinhood deliberately re-trusts the delegate internally, so an authorized delegate is not accidentally subjected to the ordinary cap.

Permissionless deleverage now skips users who are already in liquidation. Quarantine zeroes the debt-repayment deleverage routes and their maximum view, but it does not block the separate collateral-swap route. That boundary is important: quarantine is not a universal ban on every deleverage action. Existing full-payoff buffers, dust formulas, the same-block withdrawal cooldown, and swap authorization remain intact.

For callback-sensitive deleverage, retained mutation paths reject nesting. Settlement also rereads the debt after collateral interaction and reverts if the debt changed, instead of settling against a stale snapshot. Configured Stability positions are handled separately with fail-soft availability and are skipped during ordinary priority processing. Whether real collateral can invoke those callbacks, and whether the withdrawal-based integration is deployed, is still unclear.

The concise takeaway is this: deposits and withdrawals demand measured, fully backed movement; quarantine turns questionable collateral into zero usable capacity; repayment better serves the actual payer and can proceed through price trouble; redemption becomes batch-only and skips unusable targets more safely; and deleverage replaces broad integration trust with per-user authority and stronger settlement guards. Three consequential questions remain. Which governance, Stability, and priority settings are actually deployed? Which action-block source is active? And which callback-capable assets or withdrawal integrations can reach the configuration-dependent protections in practice?
